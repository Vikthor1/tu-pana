## 1. Project Overview

Tu Pana de Escritura — "Your Writing Companion" — is a bilingual Spanish/English AI writing coach designed specifically for multilingual, immigrant, Latinx, Caribbean, and working-class college students. It is a structured, process-based web application that guides students through ten stages of writing development using constrained AI interaction, reflective prompting, and metacognitive documentation.

Tu Pana de Escritura emerges from the teaching practice of a Puerto Rican, Afro-Caribbean scholar working with multilingual, Latinx, Caribbean, immigrant, and working-class students in the Bronx. Its design reflects the classroom realities of students who are often asked to enter academic writing through forms that do not always recognize their histories, languages, or intellectual resources. For that reason, the app is not only a writing support tool but also a pedagogical intervention into how AI might be redesigned around dignity, authorship, and linguistic justice.

The app was created because existing AI writing tools were designed for a general academic audience that tacitly assumes monolingual English fluency, familiarity with dominant academic conventions, and access to institutional support structures. Many students — particularly at open-access institutions, community colleges, and public universities with large immigrant and first-generation populations — navigate writing assignments while also navigating language, cultural identity, economic precarity, and institutional structures that were not designed with them in mind. Generic AI tools offered these students either uncritical language correction or, worse, the option to outsource their writing entirely.

Tu Pana responds to this gap by building an AI interlocutor that does something different: it asks questions, prompts reflection, offers structured support for drafting and revision, and keeps the student at the center of the intellectual work. The app is grounded in the conviction that multilingual, racially minoritized, and working-class students bring legitimate intellectual assets to academic writing — specifically, lived experience, family history, community memory, migration narratives, place-based knowledge, and bilingual expression — and that a writing coach should help students recognize and develop these assets rather than strip them away.

Tu Pana is not an essay generator. It does not write for students. Its purpose is to scaffold student authorship: to help students find their ideas, develop their arguments, strengthen their evidence, and reflect on their process — while keeping the writing, the thinking, and the voice entirely their own.

## 2. Core Philosophical Claim

The central philosophical claim of Tu Pana de Escritura is this: writing is a process of intellectual self-location.

Students do not merely produce text when they write. They locate themselves in relation to memory, language, community, history, and structural forces. A student writing about her grandmother's migration from Puerto Rico to New York is not simply narrating a family story. She is engaging in an act of historical consciousness: connecting the personal to the political, the intimate to the institutional, the body to the archive. The essay form — at its best — is a vehicle for this kind of thinking.

Tu Pana is built on the belief that this kind of thinking must come from the student. The AI is not the author, the authority, or the evaluator. It is a constrained interlocutor: it listens, asks, reflects back, and supports. It does not produce. It does not judge. It does not replace the student's labor with its own.

This matters pedagogically because writing is not just a communication skill. It is a mode of knowledge production. When students draft, revise, question, and reflect, they are developing the capacity to think in writing — a capacity that belongs to them and cannot be transferred by having an AI generate text on their behalf. An AI that writes the essay for the student does not develop this capacity; it simulates its product while bypassing the process that makes the product meaningful.

Tu Pana places AI in a secondary, constrained role — not because AI is dangerous (though it can be), but because the student's intellectual labor is the point. The AI is there to make that labor more supported, more scaffolded, and more visible — not to perform it.

Consider a concrete example: a student might begin with a memory of translating for a parent at a clinic, a housing office, a school meeting, or a social-service appointment. Tu Pana would not turn that memory into an essay. Instead, it would ask the student to describe the scene more specifically, identify what larger social structures were present in that moment, think about what kinds of research might deepen the analysis, and — later in the process — decide which bilingual phrases should remain in the draft because they carry the emotional and political force of the experience. The student writes. The AI asks. The thinking is the student's own.

## 3. Decolonial AI Pedagogy

Higher education writing instruction carries within it a long history of coloniality: the privileging of standardized academic English, disembodied "objective" prose, and institutionally sanctioned forms of evidence and argument. Students who write from multilingual backgrounds, from communities outside dominant cultural spaces, or from lived experiences that do not map neatly onto canonical academic frameworks are often taught that their language is imprecise, their knowledge is anecdotal, and their experience is insufficient evidence. The implicit message of conventional academic writing instruction — and now of most AI writing tools — is that students must translate themselves into a neutral, generic, elevated form in order to be heard.

Decolonial pedagogy challenges this premise. It argues that the academy's preferred forms of knowledge are not neutral but situated — produced by particular histories of power, exclusion, and institutional gatekeeping. Decolonial writing pedagogy, building on scholars like Gloria Anzaldúa, Ana Celia Zentella, Paul Kei Matsuda, Vershawn Ashanti Young, and others, asks: What would it look like to treat the student's language, memory, and experience as legitimate sites of intellectual inquiry rather than obstacles to be overcome? These names are included as intellectual reference points rather than as a formal bibliography for the audio overview.

Tu Pana de Escritura is an attempt to answer that question through the design of an AI tool.

Decolonial AI does not mean adding diverse content to a chatbot or training a model on more multilingual data. It means redesigning the role of AI in the educational encounter. It means asking: Whose thinking does the AI scaffold, and whose does it displace? Whose language does it reinforce, and whose does it flatten? Whose knowledge does it treat as valid, and whose does it frame as insufficient?

Tu Pana constrains AI precisely to prevent it from becoming another mechanism for extraction, standardization, or linguistic correction without student agency. The AI does not rewrite student sentences into standardized academic English. It does not tell students their Spanglish is wrong. It does not impose a single model of good writing. Instead, it asks students questions: What do you want to say here? What do you remember? What does your community know about this? What does this evidence mean for your argument?

This is decolonial AI pedagogy in practice: not the absence of AI, but the placement of AI in the service of student intellectual sovereignty.

## 4. The Student-Writes-First Principle

One of the most important pedagogical and ethical foundations of Tu Pana is what might be called the student-writes-first principle: at every stage of the writing process, the student must write before the AI responds.

The AI does not generate essay prose. It does not produce outlines, thesis statements, introductions, conclusions, body paragraphs, citations, self-assessments, or process reflections. When a student reaches any of these moments in the writing process, the AI's role is to help the student prepare to write — through questions, prompts, and structured support — not to produce the writing itself.

This principle exists for several interconnected reasons.

First, it protects authorship. If an AI generates an outline and the student reorganizes it slightly, or if the AI writes a thesis statement and the student submits it, the intellectual work of thinking through structure and argument has been outsourced. The student has produced a document but has not developed the capacity that document is meant to represent. The authorship is simulated, not earned.

Second, it reduces ghostwriting in a meaningful way — not through detection after the fact, but through design before the fact. The app's structure makes it difficult to use AI as a ghostwriter because the app's workflow and guardrails strictly constrain the AI from producing the core textual outputs of the assignment. The student cannot simply ask Tu Pana to write the essay and receive it.

Third, it turns AI into a dialogic tutor rather than a production machine. A good writing tutor does not write for the student. A good writing tutor asks questions, points to gaps, offers alternatives, and helps the student think. Tu Pana is designed to behave like a good writing tutor — constrained, responsive, and student-centered.

The student-writes-first principle is not a restriction on the student. It is a protection of the student's intellectual labor.

## 5. Bilingualism, Translanguaging, and Voice

Tu Pana de Escritura treats Spanish, English, Spanglish, code-switching, dialect, accent, family idioms, and culturally specific expressions as rhetorical resources — not as errors to be corrected.

The theoretical grounding for this comes from translanguaging pedagogy, which understands multilingual speakers not as users of two or more separate, bounded language systems, but as communicators who draw flexibly from a full linguistic repertoire to make meaning. A student who writes "mi abuela me decía" in the middle of an English-language analysis is not code-switching carelessly. She is making a rhetorical choice: preserving the voice, the relationship, and the cultural specificity that translation would diminish.

The app does not tell students this kind of language is wrong. It does not automatically flag bilingual constructions as errors or push students toward standardized monolingual forms. Instead, it helps students think about their own language choices: When do you want to preserve this phrase? When do you want to translate it for an audience that may not share the language? When does keeping it in Spanish — or in Spanglish — strengthen your argument, and when does it need explanation?

This is what multilingual rhetorical agency looks like in practice: not the suppression of the student's full linguistic repertoire, but the development of the student's capacity to make informed, intentional choices about how to deploy that repertoire.

**The Voice Vault** is one of the most concrete expressions of this commitment. In Stage 8 — Pulir Voz / Voice Polish — students are invited to identify specific phrases, expressions, or moments of language in their draft that should be protected during revision. These might be a phrase from a parent, a community-specific term, a code-switched sentence that carries cultural or emotional weight, or a moment of bilingual expression that is analytically precise in ways that translation would dilute. The Voice Vault is a space where the student declares: this language is deliberate. This is not an error. Do not flatten this.

The app's revision support is designed to honor the Voice Vault. Smoother is not always better. A more "polished" sentence that sounds generic, elevated, or AI-generated may be a less honest, less specific, and less intellectually grounded sentence than the original. Tu Pana helps students resist the flattening pressure of revision and develop the judgment to know when to refine and when to hold.

## 6. The Ten-Stage Writing Process

Tu Pana guides students through ten sequential stages. The stages move from personal memory toward structural analysis, drafting, revision, voice protection, and metacognitive reflection. Below is a faculty-facing description of each stage.

---

**Stage 1: Anécdota / Anecdote**

*What students do:* Students recall and narrate a specific personal or family memory related to a topic of their choice. This might be a moment of arrival, departure, discrimination, belonging, conflict, care, or survival. The anecdote is written in the student's own words, in whatever language feels right.

*What the AI does:* The AI asks clarifying questions to help the student go deeper and more specific. It might ask: Where were you? Who else was there? What did it feel like? What do you remember most clearly?

*What the AI does not do:* Write the anecdote. Suggest plot points. Impose narrative structure. Evaluate whether the memory is "good enough."

*Pedagogical purpose:* To establish the student as a narrator and a witness. To ground the writing in lived experience from the first moment, rather than asking students to begin with abstraction.

---

**Stage 2: Conexión / Connection**

*What students do:* Students begin connecting their personal anecdote to a broader social, historical, or community context. What larger forces or structures does the memory touch? Migration policy, labor exploitation, environmental racism, educational exclusion, cultural erasure — what patterns does the student begin to see?

*What the AI does:* The AI helps students ask analytical questions about their experience. It offers conceptual prompts to help students move from the personal to the structural.

*What the AI does not do:* Provide the analysis. Name the structural forces for the student. Write the transition between anecdote and analysis.

*Pedagogical purpose:* To teach students that personal experience is not merely personal — it is also social, historical, and political. This stage begins the movement from memoir toward analysis.

---

**Stage 3: Tu Pitch / Topic Pitch**

*What students do:* Students articulate what their essay is about and why it matters. This is not a formal thesis statement — it is a working claim about what the student wants to argue, explore, or understand.

*What the AI does:* The AI helps students clarify their focus by asking: What question does your essay try to answer? Who needs to hear this? What would be different if more people understood this?

*What the AI does not do:* Write the thesis. Formulate the claim. Decide the topic for the student.

*Pedagogical purpose:* To develop the student's capacity for intellectual positioning. To give students ownership of their argument before they begin researching.

---

**Stage 4: Investigación / Research**

*What students do:* Students begin locating, reading, and evaluating sources. They practice identifying relevant keywords, distinguishing source types, and connecting evidence to their topic.

*What the AI does:* The AI can help students think about what kinds of sources might be relevant, what questions to bring to a library database, how to evaluate credibility, and how different types of evidence might support different kinds of claims. It can also help students think through the ethical dimensions of citation and attribution.

*What the AI does not do:* Invent sources. Fabricate citations. Provide fake bibliographic references. Generate summaries of sources the student has not read.

*Pedagogical purpose:* To teach students that research is a form of intellectual conversation, not a data-collection task. To protect the integrity of citation by keeping the student in direct contact with real sources.

*Note:* This stage includes an AI literacy reflection checkpoint, where students are prompted to think critically about how AI tools — including Tu Pana itself — handle information, uncertainty, and evidence.

---

**Stage 5: Esquema / Outline**

*What students do:* Students draft their own organizational structure for the essay. They decide how to sequence their anecdote, analysis, research, and reflection.

*What the AI does:* The AI can help students think through organizational logic by asking questions: Where does your research connect most directly to your anecdote? Where does your argument need more support? What do you want your reader to understand by the end?

*What the AI does not do:* Generate the outline. Impose a five-paragraph structure. Organize the essay for the student.

*Pedagogical purpose:* To develop the student's rhetorical decision-making. Structure is not a neutral container — it is an argument about what matters and in what order.

---

**Stage 6: Primer Borrador / First Draft**

*What students do:* Students write a complete first draft of their essay in their own words. This is the longest and most demanding stage.

*What the AI does:* The AI offers encouragement, helps students work through blocks, and can respond to specific questions about how to move from one section to another.

*What the AI does not do:* Write any portion of the draft. Generate sentences. Complete unfinished paragraphs.

*Pedagogical purpose:* To establish that the student has produced a complete piece of writing. The first draft is the foundation of authorship; everything that follows is development, not replacement.

---

**Stage 7: Revisión / Revision**

*What students do:* Students review their draft with attention to argument, evidence, structure, specificity, and clarity. They evaluate their writing against the Five Questions framework (detailed in Section 9).

*What the AI does:* The AI offers targeted, question-based feedback on specific aspects of the draft the student chooses to focus on. This is a Flash-tier AI response — the strongest AI model in the app — because paragraph-level reasoning requires more nuanced engagement.

*What the AI does not do:* Rewrite the student's sentences. Produce a revised draft. Generate a new version of the essay.

*Pedagogical purpose:* To teach revision as thinking, not polishing. Students learn to evaluate their own writing against intellectual criteria, not merely stylistic ones.

*Note:* This stage includes an AI literacy reflection checkpoint, where students consider what the AI's feedback can and cannot do, and where its judgments should be questioned.

---

**Stage 8: Pulir Voz / Voice Polish**

*What students do:* Students review their draft for voice, language choice, and cultural specificity. They identify phrases for the Voice Vault — expressions that should be preserved and protected during final editing.

*What the AI does:* The AI helps students evaluate specific language choices and think about audience. It can ask: Does this phrase feel right to you? Is this the language you would use with someone you trust? Is this a moment you want to preserve, or a moment you want to clarify?

*What the AI does not do:* Correct the student's bilingual expression. Standardize language without student direction. Remove culturally specific language.

*Pedagogical purpose:* To protect the student's rhetorical and cultural voice through the revision process. To teach students that revision does not require self-erasure.

*Note:* This stage also includes an AI literacy reflection checkpoint.

---

**Stage 9: Checklist**

*What students do:* Students review their essay against a structured checklist covering content, structure, evidence, voice, and process documentation.

*What the AI does:* The AI can help students think through specific checklist items they are uncertain about.

*What the AI does not do:* Complete the checklist. Grade the essay. Make final decisions for the student.

*Pedagogical purpose:* To build metacognitive habit — the capacity to evaluate one's own work before submitting it. To give students a final moment of agency before the writing leaves their hands.

---

**Stage 10: Mi Cierre de Proceso / My Writing Snapshot**

*What students do:* Students complete a structured self-assessment of their writing process. They reflect on what they learned, what was difficult, what surprised them, and how their thinking developed.

*What the AI does:* After the student has written their self-reflection, the AI may offer a limited coach perspective — based only on what it observed in the student's interactions during the process — to help the student notice patterns. The student always writes first.

*What the AI does not do:* Write the student's self-assessment. Generate a process reflection on the student's behalf. Replace the student's metacognitive account with its own summary.

*Pedagogical purpose:* To close the writing process with intellectual accountability. To give students ownership of their development narrative. To produce documentation — the Instructor Process Report — that can help faculty understand the student's process, not just the product.

## 7. Research and Citation Ethics

One of the most significant ethical risks in AI-assisted academic writing is the generation of false sources. Large language models are trained to produce plausible text, and in academic contexts, plausible text often includes plausible-sounding citations: real-sounding author names, real-sounding journals, real-sounding publication years. These fabricated references pass a surface-level check but do not exist.

Tu Pana is designed to avoid this failure. The app does not generate bibliographic citations. It does not summarize sources the student has not read. It does not provide invented evidence. When students are in research-related stages, the AI's role is to help them think about the research process — what to look for, where to look, how to evaluate what they find, and how to connect evidence to their argument — but the student must locate, read, evaluate, and cite real sources.

This design choice reflects a commitment to the integrity of academic knowledge production. Research is not a database query — it is an intellectual practice that requires the researcher to engage directly with sources, evaluate their relevance and credibility, and take responsibility for how they are used. AI cannot and should not replace that practice.

When a student asks Tu Pana for a citation, the AI should redirect them toward the research process: What is your question? What keywords might help you find relevant sources? What databases or archives does your institution provide access to? What types of sources — empirical studies, historical records, journalistic accounts, community documents — might speak to your argument?

This is not a limitation of the app. It is a pedagogical commitment.

## 8. Revision Without Erasure

Revision is one of the most pedagogically contested moments in writing instruction. In conventional academic writing pedagogy, revision is often framed as a process of elevation: making the writing more formal, more polished, more institutionally legible. Students are taught to make their writing sound more "professional," more "academic," more "clear" — where clarity is implicitly defined as proximity to standardized, monolingual, disembodied academic prose.

For multilingual and racially minoritized students, this version of revision often functions as a demand for self-erasure. To revise well, in this model, is to sound less like yourself.

Tu Pana challenges this model. The app's approach to revision is guided by a simple but significant principle: **smoother is not always better.**

A sentence that has been flattened by AI editing — regularized, genericized, elevated to a kind of frictionless academic clarity — may be less honest, less specific, and less intellectually grounded than the original. The original sentence, with its bilingual inflection, its colloquial directness, its culturally specific reference, may be doing more intellectual work than its "corrected" version. It may be more precise. It may be more true.

Tu Pana helps students understand that revision is not about sounding more like an institution. It is about becoming clearer, more specific, more evidenced, and more reflective — while remaining recognizably themselves. Students learn to ask: Did I revise this because it was unclear, or because I was afraid it sounded too much like me? That question — that distinction — is one of the most important things a writing education can teach.

The Voice Vault exists to make this principle concrete. By identifying and protecting specific language choices before revision, students enter the revision process with explicit commitments to what must not be lost. They revise toward clarity and strength, not toward standardization.

## 9. The Five Questions Framework

Across multiple stages of the writing process, Tu Pana invites students to evaluate their writing — and the AI's feedback — using a Five Questions framework. These questions are not a rubric. They are heuristics for intellectual and rhetorical self-assessment.

**1. Is it accurate?**
Does this claim reflect what actually happened, what the evidence shows, or what the student genuinely understands? Is anything overstated, oversimplified, or invented?

**2. Does it preserve voice?**
Does this passage sound like the student — their way of speaking, their rhetorical choices, their cultural and linguistic identity? Or has something been flattened, corrected away, or replaced with a generic academic register?

**3. Is it specific?**
Does this sentence point to something real and particular — a name, a place, a date, a concrete detail — or is it vague and generalized? Specificity is both an intellectual virtue and a rhetorical one.

**4. Does it show thinking?**
Can a reader see the student working through a problem, considering evidence, making connections, or arriving at a claim? Or is the writing a report of conclusions without the intellectual labor that produced them?

**5. Does it honor cultural knowledge?**
Does this passage treat the student's community, family, language, and experience as legitimate sources of intellectual insight? Or has the writing moved toward institutional legibility at the cost of the knowledge it was trying to express?

This framework helps faculty understand what Tu Pana is actually doing when it provides feedback. It is not checking grammar. It is not evaluating formal correctness. It is helping students develop a form of critical self-reading that treats voice, specificity, accuracy, thinking, and cultural knowledge as the primary criteria for strong writing.

## 10. Stage 10: My Writing Snapshot and Instructor Process Report

The final stage of Tu Pana is designed to close the writing process with metacognitive accountability and instructional documentation.

**My Writing Snapshot (Mi Cierre de Proceso)** is the student's self-assessment. Students are asked to reflect on their process: What did they learn? What was most difficult? What surprised them? How did their thinking develop over the course of the stages? What would they do differently?

This reflection is the student's own. The AI must not write it. The AI must not generate a process summary and present it as the student's self-assessment. The student must produce this reflection from their own experience of the writing process.

After the student has written their reflection, the AI coach may offer a limited perspective — based only on what it observed during the student's interactions in the app — to help the student notice patterns they may not have seen themselves. This coach perspective is framed explicitly as secondary: it is one lens, not the authoritative account.

**The Instructor Process Report** is generated from the student's documented process. It is not a grade. It is not an evaluation. It is a process document: a structured account of the student's journey through the ten stages, intended to give faculty a window into process that the final product alone cannot provide. Which stages required the most work? Where did the student push through resistance? Where did the AI's support seem most useful? Where did the student redirect or reject the AI's suggestions?

The Instructor Process Report is meant to supplement instructor judgment, not replace it. Faculty who receive it should read it as process documentation — evidence of what the student did — not as a recommendation about the student's grade or standing.

This design reflects a commitment to transparency and to the pedagogical value of process. In a world where AI can generate polished text easily, the process is where human intellectual labor is most visible. Tu Pana makes that process legible.

## 11. Why This Matters for Faculty

Tu Pana de Escritura was built in response to a set of challenges that faculty at open-access, community college, and public university institutions know well.

**Post-pandemic literacy challenges.** Students who were students during the COVID-19 pandemic and its aftermath experienced significant disruption to their development as writers. Many missed years of in-person writing instruction, feedback, and peer engagement. Faculty are now working with students who need more scaffolding, more support, and more structured process engagement than previous cohorts.

**Student anxiety around writing.** Writing anxiety is widespread, and it is particularly acute among multilingual students and first-generation college students who have received explicit or implicit messages that their language is incorrect, their knowledge is insufficient, or their experiences are not academically relevant. Tu Pana is designed to meet students where they are — to treat anxiety as a signal, not a deficiency, and to offer scaffolded support rather than judgment.

**Multilingual students navigating academic norms.** The academic writing conventions taught in most institutions were designed by and for monolingual standard English speakers. Multilingual students bring rich intellectual and rhetorical resources to academic writing, but they are often penalized for the ways their language exceeds or diverges from these conventions. Tu Pana treats multilingual expression as a resource, not a problem.

**Faculty concerns about AI ghostwriting.** Since the wide public availability of large language models, faculty have expressed serious concerns about academic integrity. Can they tell whether a student's essay was written by the student or by an AI? Tu Pana addresses this concern not through detection but through design: by prohibiting the AI from generating essay prose, by documenting the student's process, and by making the writing journey visible, the app makes it structurally difficult for students to use AI as a ghostwriter — while also giving faculty a process record to consult.

**The need for process-based evidence of student authorship.** A final essay is a product. It can be evaluated for quality, but it cannot, by itself, tell a faculty member what the student actually did. Tu Pana's Instructor Process Report provides process evidence: what the student drafted, revised, and reflected on, in what order, with what kinds of support.

**The importance of teaching students how to use AI critically.** AI tools are already in students' hands. The question is not whether students will use them but how. Tu Pana provides a model for what critical, ethically grounded, pedagogically sound AI use looks like — and it teaches students to evaluate AI's outputs, question its judgments, and make their own decisions.

**The need for equity-centered AI pedagogy.** Most AI writing tools were built for a general market, not for the specific populations who need the most support. Tu Pana was built differently: for multilingual students, for Latinx and Caribbean students, for first-generation students, for students at open-access institutions. It reflects a conviction that equity cannot be an afterthought in educational AI design.

## 12. What Makes Tu Pana Different From Generic AI Tools

The table below offers a schematic comparison. It is not meant to condemn other tools — generic AI tools are not inherently bad, and they can be used well with intentional pedagogical framing. The point is that Tu Pana was designed to embed that framing in the tool itself.

| Dimension | Generic AI (e.g., ChatGPT without guardrails) | Tu Pana de Escritura |
|---|---|---|
| Process structure | None — produces output on demand | Ten sequential stages of scaffolded development |
| Authorship | Can generate full essays, outlines, theses | Prohibited from generating essay prose |
| Language policy | Defaults to standardized English | Treats bilingualism and translanguaging as resources |
| Student population | Designed for general market | Designed for multilingual, Latinx, immigrant students |
| Pedagogical grounding | None built in | Decolonial, culturally sustaining, process-based |
| Citation integrity | Can fabricate sources | Will not generate citations |
| Revision approach | May flatten voice | Revision without erasure; Voice Vault |
| Process documentation | None | Instructor Process Report |
| AI literacy | Not addressed | Embedded reflection checkpoints at key stages |
| Classroom integration | Requires instructor setup | Designed for classroom use with faculty oversight |

The fundamental difference is not a matter of model capability or interface design. It is a matter of purpose. Generic AI tools are designed to produce outputs efficiently. Tu Pana is designed to develop student thinking deliberately.

## 13. Suggested Podcast Themes for NotebookLM to Emphasize

The following themes should guide the structure and emphasis of a faculty-facing audio overview of Tu Pana de Escritura.

**AI as companion, not author.** The most important thing to communicate is what the AI does not do. Tu Pana does not write for students. It accompanies them.

**Writing as memory, history, and analysis.** Tu Pana begins with anecdote and moves toward structural analysis. This arc — from personal memory to historical consciousness — is the pedagogical spine of the app.

**Protecting student voice in the age of generative AI.** AI tools can flatten, standardize, and genericize student writing. Tu Pana is designed to resist this pressure and to help students protect what is most distinctive, honest, and culturally grounded in their voice.

**Decolonial AI pedagogy in practice.** This is not an abstract commitment. Tu Pana makes specific design decisions — about language, about structure, about what AI is and is not allowed to do — that enact a decolonial orientation.

**Multilingual writing as intellectual strength.** Faculty audiences should come away understanding that a student who writes in Spanglish, who code-switches strategically, or who preserves a family idiom in their draft is not demonstrating a deficit. They are demonstrating multilingual rhetorical agency.

**Guardrails as pedagogy, not merely restriction.** The app's constraints — the things the AI is prohibited from doing — are not limitations. They are pedagogical choices. They keep the student at the center of the intellectual work.

**Faculty concerns about AI and academic integrity.** This is the concern that most faculty bring to conversations about AI. Tu Pana addresses it directly, not by pretending AI doesn't exist, but by designing around the specific integrity risk: unauthorized ghostwriting.

**From product-centered writing to process-centered authorship.** The dominant assessment model in academic writing evaluates the product. Tu Pana makes the process visible and documentable, offering faculty a richer picture of what the student actually did.

## 14. Terms and Phrases to Use Consistently

The following terms and phrases reflect the conceptual commitments of Tu Pana de Escritura. NotebookLM hosts and generated content should use these terms consistently to maintain clarity and accuracy.

**Tu Pana de Escritura** — always use the full name on first reference; "Tu Pana" is acceptable shorthand in subsequent references.

**AI writing coach** — the preferred description of what Tu Pana is. Not "AI tutor," not "chatbot," not "writing assistant," not "AI tool" (too generic).

**Student-writes-first principle** — the foundational rule that the student must produce writing before the AI responds.

**Scaffolded authorship** — the app's central pedagogical goal: supporting the student's development as an author through structured, constrained AI interaction.

**Decolonial AI pedagogy** — the theoretical and ethical framework in which the app's design is grounded.

**Multilingual rhetorical agency** — the capacity of multilingual students to make informed, intentional choices about how to deploy their full linguistic repertoire in academic writing.

**Translanguaging** — the pedagogical and linguistic concept that describes how multilingual speakers draw from a unified linguistic repertoire rather than two separate language systems.

**Voice Vault** — the Stage 8 feature that allows students to identify and protect language that carries personal, cultural, emotional, or political meaning.

**My Writing Snapshot** — the English translation of Mi Cierre de Proceso; the Stage 10 student self-assessment.

**Instructor Process Report** — the documentation of the student's writing process generated at Stage 10; not a grade, not an evaluation.

**Guardrailed AI** — AI that has been architecturally constrained to prevent specific outputs, in this case essay prose, citations, and self-assessments.

**Process-based writing** — an approach to writing instruction that emphasizes development, revision, and reflection over the production of polished final products.

**Culturally sustaining writing pedagogy** — a pedagogical approach, drawing on Django Paris and others, that treats students' cultural knowledge, language, and experience as assets to be sustained rather than deficits to be remediated.

**Revision without erasure** — the app's approach to revision, which strengthens writing while protecting student voice and cultural knowledge.

## 15. Terms and Framings to Avoid

## 17. Longer Faculty-Facing Description (approximately 500 words)

Tu Pana de Escritura — "Your Writing Companion" — is a bilingual Spanish/English AI writing coach designed specifically for multilingual, Latinx, Caribbean, immigrant, and working-class college students. It is a structured, process-based web application that guides students through ten stages of writing development using carefully constrained AI interaction, reflective prompting, and metacognitive documentation. The app was built from a conviction that most AI writing tools were not designed for the populations who need the most support — and that the design choices embedded in those tools often replicate the same inequities that already shape higher education.

The central principle of Tu Pana is the student-writes-first rule: at every stage of the process, the student must produce writing before the AI responds. The app is designed and guardrailed to prevent the AI from generating essay prose, outlines, thesis statements, introductions, conclusions, citations, or process reflections. Its role is not to produce — it is to accompany. The app is designed to function like a constrained, ethical writing tutor: asking questions, offering structured prompts, helping students notice what their writing is doing and what it might do next, but keeping the intellectual labor squarely in the student's hands.

Tu Pana moves students through a sequence of ten stages: Anécdota (personal memory), Conexión (social and historical connection), Tu Pitch (working claim), Investigación (research and source evaluation), Esquema (student-generated outline), Primer Borrador (first draft), Revisión (revision with the Five Questions framework), Pulir Voz (voice protection and the Voice Vault), Checklist (metacognitive self-evaluation), and Mi Cierre de Proceso (process reflection and Instructor Process Report).

The app's approach to language is grounded in translanguaging pedagogy. Spanish, English, Spanglish, code-switching, family idioms, and culturally specific expressions are treated as rhetorical resources, not errors. Stage 8 — Pulir Voz — introduces the Voice Vault, where students identify phrases and expressions that must be protected during revision because they carry cultural, emotional, or political weight. The app teaches students that revision is not self-erasure: smoother is not always better.

Stage 10 closes the process with two documents: My Writing Snapshot, the student's own metacognitive self-assessment, and the Instructor Process Report, a structured documentation of the student's journey through the stages. The Process Report is not a grade — it is evidence of process, designed to give faculty a window into intellectual development that the final product alone cannot provide.

Three stages — 4, 7, and 8 — include embedded AI literacy reflection checkpoints, where students are asked to think critically about how AI tools work, what they can and cannot be trusted to do, and what it means to use AI responsibly and with clear-eyed skepticism.

Tu Pana is not a solution to the challenges of teaching writing to multilingual students. It is a pedagogical companion: one that takes seriously the intellectual richness that multilingual, immigrant, and working-class students bring to academic writing, and that tries to build an AI tool worthy of that seriousness. It is designed for faculty who want their students to write — not to generate, not to simulate, not to outsource — but to write, think, reflect, and develop as authors.

## 20. Closing Synthesis

Tu Pana de Escritura is not simply a tool for helping students finish an assignment. It is an attempt — careful, provisional, committed, and ongoing — to redesign AI-supported writing instruction around authorship, dignity, multilingual voice, historical consciousness, and critical pedagogy.

The stakes are not small. As generative AI becomes more capable and more available, educational institutions face a genuine choice about what role AI will play in how students learn to write. If AI is allowed to produce essays on demand — if the efficiency of generation is treated as a pedagogical virtue — then writing instruction risks becoming the management of AI output rather than the development of human capacity. Students become curators rather than authors. They learn to prompt rather than to think.

Tu Pana makes a different bet: that students — including students who have been told their language is incorrect, their knowledge is insufficient, and their experience is not academically legitimate — are capable of serious intellectual work, and that an AI tool built around that conviction can help more of them do it.

This is a pedagogical and ethical claim, not a technological one. Tu Pana is not powerful because of its model or its interface. It is meaningful because of its commitments: to the student as author, to the instructor as pedagogue, to bilingualism as a resource, to process as evidence, to revision as development rather than erasure, and to AI as a constrained, accountable participant in the educational encounter — not its replacement.

The field of AI-assisted writing instruction is still being built. The choices made now — about what AI is allowed to do, what it is constrained from doing, whose students it is designed for, and what pedagogical values are built into its architecture — will shape how an entire generation of students learns to write. Tu Pana is one answer to those questions. It is offered not as the only answer, but as proof that the questions can be asked with care, and that the answers can be built with integrity.
