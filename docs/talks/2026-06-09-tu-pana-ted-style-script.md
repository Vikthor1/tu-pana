# AI Should Not Replace Student Voice. It Should Help Students Hear It.

*TED-style script — Tu Pana de Escritura*
*Drafted: 2026-06-09 | Target audience: faculty, administrators, HSI stakeholders, ed-tech leaders*
*Approximate length: 850 words / ~6 minutes at spoken pace*

---

Imagine a student sitting alone at 11 PM. The assignment is due tomorrow. She knows the story she wants to tell — about her grandmother leaving Puerto Rico, about the apartment in the Bronx where three languages moved through the kitchen, about what it means to be the first in her family to sit in a college classroom. She knows all of that. She has been living it her whole life.

But the cursor blinks. And nothing comes.

This is not a story about a student who isn't ready for college. This is a story about a system that wasn't designed to meet her where she is.

---

For decades, we have treated the blank page as evidence of something missing in the student. But what is often missing is not intelligence, not experience, not ideas. What is missing is a responsive presence — someone who asks the right question at the right moment. *What are you trying to say? Why does this matter to you? What do you already know that your reader needs to know?*

Writing is hard for everyone. But it is especially hard when you are doing it at midnight after a shift. It is harder when the academic voice you are asked to perform doesn't sound like the voice that raised you. It is harder when you have been told — implicitly or explicitly — that your language, your Spanish, your Spanglish, your Caribbean cadences, does not belong on the page.

---

At Hispanic-Serving Institutions — colleges where at least 25 percent of enrolled students identify as Hispanic or Latino — this is not a marginal experience. It is the central one.

These students bring something extraordinary into the classroom. Multilingual minds. Migration histories. Family economies. Cultural knowledge that no textbook can replicate. They navigate between languages not because they are confused, but because they are intellectually richer than any single language can contain.

What they often lack is not ability. It is infrastructure. The structured, responsive support that helps students move from thinking to writing to revising — that infrastructure is uneven, underfunded, and often unavailable at the moments students need it most.

---

That is the problem Tu Pana de Escritura was designed to address.

Tu Pana — where *pana* means a trusted friend, a close companion, a peer you can count on in Caribbean Spanish — is a bilingual AI writing coach. It works in Spanish, in English, and in the space between them.

And here is what it does not do: it does not write for you.

Tu Pana asks questions. When a student is stuck, it doesn't generate a paragraph. It asks: *What moment do you want the reader to feel first? What do you know that your reader doesn't?* It guides students through ten structured stages — from the first spark of an anecdote, through research and drafting, to revision and reflection. At every stage, the student writes. The AI accompanies.

Consider a student named Carlos. He's writing about urban food deserts for a sociology class. He has lived this — grew up without a supermarket in walking distance, watched his mother take two buses to buy fresh vegetables. But when he sits down to write, he can't find the academic angle. Tu Pana doesn't write his introduction. It asks him what his mother knew about that neighborhood that city planners didn't. That question unlocks the paragraph.

That is accompaniment. Not replacement.

---

We are at a critical moment in the relationship between AI and education. Many tools on the market promise to help students write faster. What they actually do is write instead of them. And when AI writes the essay, the student learns nothing — except, perhaps, how to disappear behind someone else's words.

Tu Pana makes a different bet. It bets that students already have the ideas, the experiences, the intellectual raw material. They need a structured, responsive presence to help them organize, question, and articulate what they already know. This is not a simplified version of AI support. This is a more honest and more demanding version of it.

---

For students at HSIs, the stakes are even higher. When a tool is designed from the beginning with a particular kind of student in mind — a student whose languages and histories are treated as intellectual resources, not obstacles — it changes what becomes possible.

Tu Pana does not tell students their Spanglish is wrong. It does not smooth away the roughness of a voice still finding its register. It includes a feature called the Voice Vault, where students protect the phrases that are most distinctly theirs before revision begins. And it asks students to evaluate every AI response before accepting it.

That is critical AI literacy. Not as an add-on. As the architecture.

---

We talk a great deal about what AI can do. We spend less time asking what it should refuse to do.

Tu Pana refuses to write your story for you. It insists that you are the author. It makes you revise your own words, evaluate your own argument, defend your own thinking.

In a moment when students are being told — by systems and algorithms and economics — that their voices don't belong, we need technologies that do the opposite. Technologies that say: *Your ideas are not broken. Your experience is not a deficit. Your language is not a barrier. It is the resource.*

The student sitting at 11 PM — cursor blinking, story waiting — she had it all along.

Tu Pana just helps her hear it.

---

## Post-Script Notes

**Estimated word count:** ~850 words (~6 minutes at a comfortable spoken pace of 140 wpm)

**Rhetorical strategy:**

- *Circular narrative frame:* The unnamed student opens and closes the script — the same figure, but transformed by the argument between those two moments. The ending does not introduce new information; it redeems the opening.
- *Contrast architecture:* Every section is built on what Tu Pana does *not* do before asserting what it does. This prevents the script from sounding like marketing and keeps the claims grounded.
- *Central refrain:* "Accompaniment. Not replacement." and the closing line "Tu Pana just helps her hear it" echo the title's thesis without restating it.
- *Imagined student scenarios:* The unnamed woman (opening/closing) provides emotional continuity; Carlos provides a concrete, specific pedagogical illustration. Neither is sentimentalized.
- *Anaphora and epistrophe:* "Multilingual minds. Migration histories. Family economies." (HSI section) — short declarative accumulation rather than a list. "Not as an add-on. As the architecture." — closure by compression.
- *Modal hedging:* "may," "can," "has the potential to" used throughout in line with pilot status; the script avoids claiming proven outcomes while remaining persuasive.
- *Deficit-language avoidance:* Students are framed throughout as people who bring resources, not people who lack preparation.

**Three optional alternative titles:**

1. *"The Coach That Never Writes Your Essay"* — direct, provocative, searchable
2. *"Built for Students Who Think in More Than One Language"* — foregrounds the translingual design principle
3. *"Accompaniment, Not Replacement: What AI in Education Could Mean"* — slower, more scholarly register; useful for conference or journal contexts
